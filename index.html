Hyderabad’s old city · 1999–2023
Finding Still true? The record Evidence Limits Data
Assembly elections 1999–2023 · Census 2011 · 108 GHMC wards

Held for a generation. Poor for a generation.

AIMIM has won the old city’s five assembly seats in every election it entered since 1999. The people who live there are still the poorest in Hyderabad.

Bahadurpura, Chandrayangutta, Charminar, Malakpet, Yakutpura. Six elections, one winner. Over the same years, Census 2011 found the households of these constituencies at half the cooking gas, half the internet and a third of the car ownership of the rest of their own district. In Charminar’s poorest ward, 2.3% of households had a computer with internet. In Ameerpet, across town, 47.5% did.

It is not that the state skipped the old city’s infrastructure. Water, drainage and bathrooms have the same coverage as everywhere else in the district. Whatever explains how far behind these households are, it is not missing pipes, and that closes off the easiest excuse. The sections below set out the election record, the living-standards gap, what has changed since 2011, and what this evidence can and cannot prove.

The finding

The gap is in how people live, not in what was built.

Census 2011 covered 108 GHMC wards in Hyderabad district: 52 in the six old-city mandals, 56 elsewhere. On every measure of household living standards the old city is far behind. On every measure of municipal service delivery it is level.

The spread inside the district is enormous, and it falls along the old-city line. The table below puts the poorest Charminar ward beside a ward in the Ameerpet–Punjagutta belt. These are neighbourhoods a few kilometres apart, in the same city, counted in the same census.

Two details worth holding onto. The worst-served ward in the district on treated water (49.1%) and closed drainage (49.8%) is ward 1 in Musheerabad, which is not in the old city. And the clearest single case of the whole pattern is in Bahadurpura.

Projecting forward

Does a 2011 census still describe 2026?

Partly. The service numbers and the living-standards numbers age in different ways, for reasons that come down to arithmetic.

The service parity still holds

In 2011 both halves of the district were already between 94% and 99% on every municipal measure. Coverage that high has nowhere to fall and almost nowhere to rise, so no gap can have opened since. NFHS-5 (2019–21) puts the whole district at 99.6% for improved drinking water and 99.9% for electricity, which fits.

The cooking-gas gap has closed

NFHS-5 found 99.5% of the district’s households on clean cooking fuel by 2019–21. The old city is about half the district, so it cannot be below 99% — the other half would have to be above 100%. The 21-point gap of 2011 is gone, and Ward 43’s 22.0% with it.

The banking gap has probably closed

Jan Dhan accounts opened from 2014, and by 2019–21 84% of women in Telangana had a bank account of their own. That is a different measure from the Census household figure, so it does not settle the question at ward level, but a 17-point gap is unlikely to have survived it.

Internet and vehicles are another matter

These stood at 9.1% and 5.9% in the old city in 2011. No government scheme hands out cars or computers; they follow income. Nobody has measured either at ward level since, and there is no reason to assume the distance has closed.

What closed those gaps

Ujjwala (2016) and Jan Dhan (2014). Both are central schemes, delivered nationally, everywhere at once. The old city’s cooking-gas and banking gaps closed because Delhi ran a programme, not because anything changed locally. Where no scheme reaches — assets, connectivity, the things bought with income — there is nothing on record to suggest the old city has caught up.

How firm is this section

The ceiling argument on municipal services needs no new data and is as strong as the 2011 figures themselves. The LPG conclusion rests on a single district-level figure plus a bounding argument, and carries three caveats: NFHS “clean fuel” is a broader category than the Census’s “LPG/PNG”; the bound assumes ward counts are a reasonable proxy for population shares; and NFHS is a sample survey with sampling error, where the Census is a complete enumeration.

The NFHS-5 district figures are confirmed against the official IIPS District Fact Sheet: Hyderabad (a copy is in this repository): electricity 99.9%, improved water 99.6%, sanitation 84.4%, clean cooking fuel 99.5%. The factsheet carries no NFHS-4 comparison for this district — Telangana’s district reorganisation made 2015–16 estimates non-comparable — so no district-level trend can be quoted. The bank-account figure is from the official Telangana state factsheet (84.4%; urban 83.0%), as the district sheet does not carry it. The banking statement is deliberately weaker than the LPG one, and is offered as an inference rather than a measurement.

Malakpet is the exception. AIMIM did not field a candidate there in 1999 or 2004, and BJP took the seat with 53.2%, then Congress with 52.1%. Those are the only two contests in the series AIMIM did not lead. It has held all five seats without a break since 2009.

The margins are narrowing. Since 2014 AIMIM’s share has fallen in four of the five seats. The largest fall is in Yakutpura, from 45.8% to 32.9%, on the district’s lowest turnout that year (39.6%). Chandrayangutta is the one seat where its share rose, by 5.7 points.

Yakutpura 2023 was nearly a tie: AIMIM 32.9%, MBT 32.2%. Most of AIMIM’s fall there is MBT’s recovery.

The evidence

Three views of a narrowing hold.

AIMIM’s share has fallen in four of five seats since 2014

Bahadurpura begins in 2014: the seat was created in the 2008 delimitation, and its 2009 row is missing from this dataset. Malakpet begins in 2009, the first cycle AIMIM contested it. A gap in a line means the party did not contest, not that it scored zero.

BJP’s rise is in the two seats it used to skip entirely

BJP’s highest share in this series is its 53.2% in Malakpet in 1999, so it did not grow from nothing. What changed is where it competes: absent from Chandrayangutta and Charminar until 2009, it reached 26.8% in Charminar by 2023, over the same years in which AIMIM fell there from 73.5% to 50.1%.

In 2023 the challenge is a different party in almost every seat

BJP in Charminar, Congress in Malakpet, MBT in Yakutpura, BRS in Bahadurpura. No single party opposes AIMIM across the old city, and the opposition vote splits several ways in every seat.

Interpretation

What this supports, and what it does not.

Supported

The old city has been led by one party in every contest that party entered, across six cycles, and has held all five seats continuously since 2009.

In 2011 those same neighbourhoods were statistically indistinguishable from the rest of their district on every municipal service measure available, and far behind on every household economic measure available.

The service-parity finding is robust to the age of the census, because both groups were already near the ceiling when it was taken.

Not supported

That AIMIM caused the poverty.

The data shows two things happening in the same place over the same years. To show one caused the other you would need places that differed in who represented them, and there are almost none here: AIMIM lost no contest it entered.

That the old city was denied infrastructure.

The Census says the opposite. Anyone arguing under-provision of municipal services will be refuted by the data on this page.

That an MLA could have changed either.

Urban infrastructure runs through the municipal corporation, state departments and central schemes. MLA funds are about ₹3 crore a year, small next to municipal capital budgets, and through this whole period the state and GHMC were held by TDP, Congress, then TRS/BRS. This weakens the case for blaming an MLA for the poverty, and equally the case for crediting one with the pipes.

That the direction runs one way.

Poverty shapes voting as much as voting shapes poverty. Poor, dense, old neighbourhoods hold onto parties that speak for them. Nothing in this data says which way the arrow runs.

The form of the argument that holds up

Say “the old city has no development” and this Census table refutes you by lunchtime, because the roads, water and drains are all there. The claim that holds is different: the old city has voted for the same party for a generation, and its people are still the poorest in the city — in neighbourhoods where the basic services were built long ago. The service parity is not a defence of anyone’s record. It removes the one excuse that would explain the poverty as neglect from outside, and leaves the harder question of why full pipes and full drains sit over empty bank accounts.

If services are level and lives are not, whatever binds these households is somewhere else: land title, credit, schooling, work. Three of those four now have ward-level measures. Schooling is modestly behind and work participation seven points behind at any age cut. The tenure proxy comes back level: the share of people in GHMC-listed slums is statistically the same in the old city as outside it (medians 20.5% against 19.7%, p = 0.61) — the old city’s poverty is not slum housing waiting for regularisation, it is poverty in ordinary serviced homes. Credit and land title proper stay unmeasured; RBI’s credit data stops at the district and title records are not public. A vote-share chart settles none of it, but it does establish who was in the room the whole time.

Limits of the comparison

  • Female work participation measures norms as well as opportunity. A low rate reflects who seeks work, not only who can find it. And one schooling result runs against the deprivation story: the male–female literacy gap is smaller in the old city (6.3 points vs 8.3, p = 0.0008).
  • The wards differ in composition. The old city has a much lower Scheduled Caste share (3.1% vs 7.7%) and a younger population; neither is controlled for.
  • Mandals are not constituencies. The six old-city mandals approximate the five assembly seats, but the boundaries are not identical. This compares parts of a city, not electorates.
  • These are levels, not change. Levels largely record history; a poor area in 2011 may have been poor in 1951. Detecting whether representation changed a trajectory needs at least two ward-level time points, which do not exist — census ward boundaries are renumbered between rounds and do not correspond.
  • No controls. Old-city wards differ in density, housing-stock age, unauthorised construction and land-title status, all of which predict economic outcomes independently of who represents them.
  • One census. “Sustained” on the poverty side rests on a single time point. The forward projection above bounds part of that problem; it does not remove it.

How the stronger claim could actually be established

To prove that AIMIM’s wins did little for these constituencies, you would need somewhere similar that voted differently, and there isn’t one — the party lost no contest it entered. There is also an awkward fact for the claim: services are the one thing AIMIM’s people directly ran, through its bloc of GHMC corporators, and services are the one thing that reached parity. Household income, where the real shortfall is, is what an MLA touches least.

What can be tested today is what the officeholders did with the money they controlled. The MP fund record is poor. In the 16th Lok Sabha the Hyderabad MP used about ₹7.1 crore of a ₹25 crore entitlement; in the 17th, 8 of the 69 recommended works were completed. The parliamentary record is strong: 60 debates against a national average of 46.7, 366 questions against 210, three private member’s bills against 1.5 (PRS, 17th LS). And the same under-spending shows up in every party’s MLAs: in 2017–18 Hyderabad’s sixteen legislators together spent under ₹10 crore of the ₹36 crore released to them, and the bottom five included BJP and TRS names next to AIMIM’s. The funds went unspent across the city, whoever held the seat. For a proper causal test — comparing close AIMIM wins against close losses nationally, or using the 2008 boundary redraw — see ANALYSIS.md.

The full series.

Before reading the numbers

  • Bahadurpura has no 1999–2009 rows. The seat was created in the 2008 delimitation, but it was contested in 2009 and won by AIMIM. That row is missing here, so any 2009 district total understates AIMIM.
  • Rows do not sum to 100%. There is no column for independents, smaller parties or NOTA. The residual runs from 0 to 15.6 points with a median of 3.5; twenty-one of the twenty-seven rows are under 6.
  • A blank is “did not contest”, not zero. Treating blanks as zeros will invent declines that did not happen.
  • Figures are good to about ±1 point. Spot-checks differ slightly from other sources, most likely a valid-votes versus votes-polled denominator difference.
  • The 2008 delimitation redrew these boundaries, so pre- and post-2009 rows are not strictly the same geographies.
  • “Led” is not quite “won”. This dataset tracks six parties. Leading among those six is decisive wherever the leader clears 50%, which covers both non-AIMIM contests, but for a handful of rows an untracked independent cannot be formally ruled out.
