Finding Still true? The record Evidence Limits Data
Assembly elections 1999–2023 · Census 2011 · 108 GHMC wards

The pipes went in. The income didn’t.

Sustained representation, sustained poverty — and a finding that cuts against the argument it was collected to make.

Five assembly seats in Hyderabad’s old city — Bahadurpura, Chandrayangutta, Charminar, Malakpet and Yakutpura — have returned AIMIM in almost every contest since 1999. The usual charge against that record is that the old city has been left without development.

The Census says otherwise. On water, drainage and sanitation these wards are level with the rest of Hyderabad district, and on one measure marginally ahead. The gap is in how households live, not in what the corporation built. That was the picture in 2011. Fifteen years on, two of the four economic gaps have closed — and not because of anything that happened locally.

The finding

Level on the pipes. Far behind on everything the pipes were meant to bring.

Census 2011 enumerated 108 GHMC wards in Hyderabad district. Comparing the 52 wards in the six old-city mandals against the 56 elsewhere splits the indicators cleanly in two — and not where an under-provision argument would put the split.

The single worst-served ward in the district on treated water (49.1%) and on closed drainage (49.8%) is ward 1, Musheerabad — not in the old city. And the sharpest illustration of the whole pattern sits in Bahadurpura.

Projecting forward

Does a 2011 census still describe 2026?

Partly — and which part matters. The two families of indicators age very differently, and the difference is not a matter of judgement but of arithmetic.

The service finding is ceiling-bounded, so it holds

In 2011 both groups already sat between 94% and 99% on every municipal measure. You cannot open a meaningful gap from 97 versus 97 when the ceiling is 100, and piped coverage does not run backwards. NFHS-5 (2019–21) puts Hyderabad district at 99.6% for an improved drinking-water source and 99.9% for electricity, consistent with saturation in both halves. Nothing that has happened since 2011 could have turned service parity into a service deficit.

One economic gap has demonstrably closed

The LPG gap has not survived. NFHS-5 records 99.5% of Hyderabad district households using clean cooking fuel in 2019–21. Since the old city is roughly half the district, it cannot sit below about 99% without the other half exceeding 100%. The twenty-one point gap of 2011 is arithmetically gone — and Ward 43’s 22.0% with it.

A second has probably closed

Household banking is very likely in the same position. Jan Dhan accounts opened from 2014, and by 2019–21 84% of women in Telangana had a bank account they themselves used. That is a different unit and a different definition from the Census household measure, so it does not settle the question at ward level — but a seventeen-point household gap surviving that expansion is hard to credit.

Two have no ceiling and no scheme behind them

Computer-with-internet and vehicle ownership stood at 9.1% and 5.9% in the old city in 2011, with no ceiling anywhere near and no national programme driving them towards universality. These are the measures where the economic distance plausibly persists, and they are the ones nobody has measured at ward level since.

Which sharpens the question rather than dissolving it

Note what closed the two gaps that closed. Ujjwala from 2016 and Jan Dhan from 2014 are centrally sponsored schemes, delivered nationally and uniformly. They did not close the old city’s gap because the old city was represented well or badly; they closed it everywhere at once. So convergence on cooking fuel and bank accounts is not evidence of local development, any more than the 2011 shortfall was evidence of local neglect. Both were set elsewhere. The measures still plausibly diverging are precisely the ones no scheme delivers — durable assets and connectivity, the things that follow income rather than eligibility.

How firm is this section

The ceiling argument on municipal services needs no new data and is as strong as the 2011 figures themselves. The LPG conclusion rests on a single district-level figure plus a bounding argument, and carries three caveats: NFHS “clean fuel” is a broader category than the Census’s “LPG/PNG”; the bound assumes ward counts are a reasonable proxy for population shares; and NFHS is a sample survey with sampling error, where the Census is a complete enumeration.

The NFHS-5 district figures are confirmed against the official IIPS District Fact Sheet: Hyderabad (a copy is in this repository): electricity 99.9%, improved water 99.6%, sanitation 84.4%, clean cooking fuel 99.5%. The factsheet carries no NFHS-4 comparison for this district — Telangana’s district reorganisation made 2015–16 estimates non-comparable — so no district-level trend can be quoted. The bank-account figure is from the official Telangana state factsheet (84.4%; urban 83.0%), as the district sheet does not carry it. The banking statement is deliberately weaker than the LPG one, and is offered as an inference rather than a measurement.

Malakpet is the exception, and it matters. AIMIM did not contest it in 1999 or 2004; BJP took it with 53.2% and Congress with 52.1%, both outright majorities. Those are the only two contests in this series that AIMIM did not lead. Every seat it has actually contested, it has led — and it has held all five continuously since 2009.

The dominance is real but narrowing. Since 2014 AIMIM’s share has fallen in four of the five seats. The clearest movement is in Yakutpura, from 45.8% to 32.9% — its weakest recorded position, on the district’s lowest turnout that year (39.6%). Chandrayangutta is the only seat where the party gained, up 5.7 points.

Yakutpura 2023 was effectively a tie. AIMIM 32.9%, MBT 32.2%. Reading AIMIM’s fall there without MBT’s revival misses the mechanism entirely.

The evidence

Three views of a narrowing hold.

AIMIM’s share has fallen in four of five seats since 2014

Bahadurpura begins in 2014: the seat was created in the 2008 delimitation, and its 2009 row is missing from this dataset. Malakpet begins in 2009, the first cycle AIMIM contested it. A gap in a line means the party did not contest, not that it scored zero.

BJP’s rise is in the two seats it used to skip entirely

BJP’s highest share anywhere in this series is its 53.2% in Malakpet in 1999, so the familiar “grew from nothing” story is wrong as stated. What is true is narrower and more interesting: BJP was absent from Chandrayangutta and Charminar until 2009, and it is there that it has since climbed, reaching 26.8% in Charminar by 2023 as AIMIM fell from 73.5% (2004) to 50.1%.

In 2023 the challenge is a different party in almost every seat

BJP in Charminar, Congress in Malakpet, MBT in Yakutpura, BRS in Bahadurpura. There is no single opposition to AIMIM in the old city, which is a large part of why it keeps winning: the challenging vote splits several ways in every seat.

Interpretation

What this supports, and what it does not.

Supported

The old city has been led by one party in every contest that party entered, across six cycles, and has held all five seats continuously since 2009.

In 2011 those same neighbourhoods were statistically indistinguishable from the rest of their district on every municipal service measure available, and far behind on every household economic measure available.

The service-parity finding is robust to the age of the census, because both groups were already near the ceiling when it was taken.

Not supported

That AIMIM caused the poverty.

This is a coincidence of two long-run facts. Causal inference needs variation in representation, and there is almost none — two contests, in one seat, both before the delimitation, is not an identification strategy.

That the old city was denied infrastructure.

The Census says the opposite. Anyone arguing under-provision of municipal services will be refuted by the data on this page.

That an MLA could have changed either.

Urban infrastructure runs through the municipal corporation, state departments and centrally sponsored schemes. MLA-LADS is around ₹3 crore a year, small against municipal capital budgets. Across this whole period GHMC and the state were held by TDP, Congress, then TRS/BRS. That cuts both ways: it weakens a blame argument and a credit argument equally.

That the direction runs one way.

Poor, dense, historically under-invested neighbourhoods produce distinctive political attachments. Representation may be as much consequence as cause, and nothing here separates the two.

Why the reframing is the stronger argument

“The old city has no development” is refutable in one afternoon with a single Census table, and it aims at the one thing an MLA has least control over. “A generation of unbroken representation has coincided with a generation of unchanged relative poverty, in a place where the pipes and drains were built” is harder to dismiss and more interesting. It moves the question from civic works — where the old city is doing fine — to economic mobility, where the gap was large, consistent across four indicators, and statistically unambiguous.

It also raises the question worth actually asking. If service delivery is level and outcomes are not, the binding constraint is somewhere other than pipes: land title, credit access, formal employment, schooling. None of those is settled by a vote-share chart.

Limits of the comparison

  • Mandals are not constituencies. The six old-city mandals approximate the five assembly seats, but the boundaries are not identical. This compares parts of a city, not electorates.
  • These are levels, not change. Levels largely record history; a poor area in 2011 may have been poor in 1951. Detecting whether representation changed a trajectory needs at least two ward-level time points, which do not exist — census ward boundaries are renumbered between rounds and do not correspond.
  • No controls. Old-city wards differ in density, housing-stock age, unauthorised construction and land-title status, all of which predict economic outcomes independently of who represents them.
  • One census. “Sustained” on the poverty side rests on a single time point. The forward projection above bounds part of that problem; it does not remove it.

How the stronger claim could actually be established

The claim people usually want from this material is that AIMIM’s wins have done little for these constituencies. Nothing here establishes that, and the obstacle is not data volume — it is that there is no variation in representation to compare against. Worth stating plainly: the claim is weakest exactly where the party had most power. Municipal service delivery is the corporation’s domain, AIMIM has held a large bloc of GHMC corporator seats across these wards for decades, and on those measures the old city is at parity or ahead. Household income, where the shortfall is real, is what an MLA influences least.

Three routes would do better. A close-election regression discontinuity pooling every AIMIM contest nationally — Telangana, Maharashtra, the Bihar Seemanchal seats — comparing bare wins against bare losses; the catch is that ward-level data for large cities is excluded, so it would identify an effect everywhere except Hyderabad. The 2008 delimitation moved wards between constituencies and offers within-ward variation, if the 2001 and 2011 ward identifiers can be reconciled geographically. And before either, a much cheaper test of the folk claim — which has now been run. The MPLADS record is genuinely poor: in the 16th Lok Sabha the Hyderabad MP utilised about ₹7.1 crore of a ₹25 crore entitlement, and in the 17th, 8 of 69 recommended works were completed. The legislative record points the other way: 60 debates against a national average of 46.7, 366 questions against 210, three private member’s bills against 1.5 (PRS, 17th LS). And the state-side fund shows the same under-spending across every party — in 2017–18 Hyderabad’s sixteen MLAs together spent under ₹10 crore of ₹36 crore released, the lowest spenders including BJP and TRS names alongside AIMIM’s. What survives is narrow: the discretionary works funds were substantially under-spent, by the city’s officeholders of every party.

Data and method

The full series.

Before reading the numbers

  • Bahadurpura has no 1999–2009 rows. The seat was created in the 2008 delimitation, but it was contested in 2009 and won by AIMIM. That row is missing here, so any 2009 district total understates AIMIM.
  • Rows do not sum to 100%. There is no column for independents, smaller parties or NOTA. The residual runs from 0 to 15.6 points with a median of 3.5; twenty-one of the twenty-seven rows are under 6.
  • A blank is “did not contest”, not zero. Treating blanks as zeros will invent declines that did not happen.
  • Figures are good to about ±1 point. Spot-checks differ slightly from other sources, most likely a valid-votes versus votes-polled denominator difference.
  • The 2008 delimitation redrew these boundaries, so pre- and post-2009 rows are not strictly the same geographies.
  • “Led” is not quite “won”. This dataset tracks six parties. Leading among those six is decisive wherever the leader clears 50%, which covers both non-AIMIM contests, but for a handful of rows an untracked independent cannot be formally ruled out.
